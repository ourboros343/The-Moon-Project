MISSION:
MOON SURVIVAL

72 hours to keep four astronauts alive after a meteor impact destroys oxygen production, damages the solar array, and interrupts communication with Earth.

Crew: 4 Lunar south pole Resupply window: 72 hours
Commander’s priority
Keep the habitat sealed, powered, and occupied. Do not risk a five-kilometer trip to an unverified shelter while the station remains habitable.
01 / Know your environment

Plan for the Moon you actually have.

Every environmental fact below connects to a specific survival decision.

Gravity

About one-sixth of Earth’s

The Moon’s surface gravity is about 1.62 m/s², roughly 0.165 of Earth’s. Suited crew may move awkwardly, bounce, lose balance, or disturb dust.

Plan: Avoid unnecessary surface work. For essential repairs, use handholds, slow movements, and a buddy system. Low gravity does not make a long hike easy.

Atmosphere & pressure

No breathable air outside

The Moon’s extremely thin exosphere cannot provide breathable oxygen or Earth-like pressure. A habitat leak can cause rapid loss of pressure and breathable gas.

Plan: Isolate damaged sections and continuously monitor pressure, oxygen delivery, and carbon-dioxide removal.

Temperature

Extreme heat and cold

Near the lunar equator, surface temperatures can exceed 121°C (250°F) in daylight and fall to about −133°C (−208°F) at night. Permanently shadowed polar craters can be colder than −246°C (−410°F).

Plan: Preserve habitat insulation and thermal control; do not expose people or critical equipment to the surface extremes.

Radiation

Little natural shielding

The Moon has hardly any atmosphere and no global magnetic field to shield its surface from space radiation. Solar particle events can create increased exposure risk.

Plan: Move to the habitat’s most shielded interior area during an alert. Use available water and supplies as additional shielding around the shelter.

Lunar dust

Jagged, abrasive, clingy

Lunar regolith can cling to suits and coat equipment. Abrasive dust can foul seals and mechanisms, damage sensitive surfaces, and become a crew health concern.

Plan: Treat the airlock as a dust-control zone; keep suits and tools there and avoid opening the habitat.

Decision rule

Stay while the base is safe

The station has pressure, life support, stored resources, and an emergency shelter. The distant shelter’s condition, route, and supplies are unconfirmed.

Plan: Remain at the station while it is habitable. Consider evacuation only if measurements show it cannot sustain the crew.

Life-support reminder: Oxygen reserves alone cannot keep the crew alive. Cabin pressure, carbon-dioxide removal, thermal control, and system monitoring must also keep working.

Oxygen: 4 astronauts × 0.84 kg/person/day × 3 days = 10.08 kg. From 11 kg, 0.92 kg remains.

Water: 4 × 3 L/person/day × 3 days = 36 L. From 45 L, 9 L remains.

Electricity: 1.0 kW × 72 h = 72 kWh. Backup solar: 0.3 kW × 8 h/day × 3 days = 7.2 kWh. With the 60 kWh battery, total modeled supply is 67.2 kWh, a 4.8 kWh deficit.

The backup panels provide their stated 8 hours of output on each of the three days. Reduce nonessential demand by at least 4.8 kWh, and preserve an additional reserve.

Food: The stated five-day supply nominally covers the 72-hour wait.

Conservation: Shut down nonessential lab instruments and lighting; defer experiments; reduce noncritical computing; schedule equipment use during solar-output hours. Keep oxygen delivery, CO₂ removal, pressure monitoring, thermal control, radiation alarms, and emergency communications powered. Do not conserve by withholding required oxygen, water, or medical care.
03 / The 72-hour survival strategy

Stabilize. Measure. Reassess.

Use system readings and checklists. The scheduled resupply is an opportunity, not a guarantee.

HOURS 0–6
Immediate response

Protect the crew and stop further losses

  • Account for all four crew; assess injuries, pressure, oxygen, CO₂, temperature, fire, and electrical status.
  • Isolate the damaged oxygen-production area and any leaking or structurally compromised module.
  • Confirm stored oxygen delivery, CO₂ scrubber function, pressure controls, and alarms using independent readings if available.
  • Move to the radiation shelter if detector readings indicate a solar-particle threat; place water and other available supplies around the occupied area.
  • Shut down nonessential lab loads and begin a power budget.
HOURS 6–24
Stabilize

Conserve and attempt contact safely

  • Log oxygen, water, battery state, solar input, pressure, CO₂, and temperature at regular intervals.
  • Use backup solar only if panels, wiring, and connections are safe; measure actual energy delivered.
  • Check communications power, connections, antenna alignment, and routing. Reboot only according to procedure; test backup radio if available.
  • Send short repeated emergency messages with crew status, location, remaining supplies, and resupply window; record attempts.
  • Maintain rest and low-exertion routines without reducing required oxygen delivery.
HOURS 24–48
Maintain

Keep critical systems operating

  • Compare actual power use and solar generation with the budget; shed more noncritical loads if needed.
  • Monitor CO₂ removal, pressure, oxygen use, and temperature. Investigate abnormal readings promptly.
  • During radiation alerts, shelter inside and limit time near exposed walls.
  • Try communications checks from inside when possible.
  • Reassess whether the station remains safer than evacuation.
HOURS 48–72
Prepare

Prepare for rescue and delay

  • Keep signaling equipment ready and verify any available resupply or landing information.
  • Preserve an energy margin for life support and communications; charge when backup solar is available and safe.
  • Prepare the airlock and landing approach without unnecessary surface excursions.
  • Continue food, water, and medical checks; plan for a short extension if resupply is delayed.
  • If pressure or life support becomes unstable, follow the evacuation checklist and compare measured risks with the verified route and destination.
Situation 1 · Oxygen crisis

Conserve without compromising safety

Isolate leaks, use stored oxygen as designed, and track flow and reserve. Avoid unnecessary exertion and opening the habitat. Do not reduce oxygen below safe levels; pressure control and CO₂ removal are also essential.

Situation 2 · Power crisis

Protect essential loads

Prioritize life support, pressure and leak monitoring, thermal regulation, medical equipment, radiation alarms, and communications. Shed science payloads, nonessential lights, and convenience systems.

Situation 3 · Radiation threat

Shelter inside

Move the crew to the most shielded interior location, ideally with available water and supplies around it. Stay sheltered until monitoring indicates the threat has passed.

Situation 4 · Communications

Try communications in layers

Check power, connectors, antenna alignment, and routing; test backup equipment or a preplanned relay if one exists. Send repeated concise emergency bursts and log every attempt.

Situation 5 · The shelter five kilometers away: Stay at the station while it remains pressurized and life support is stable. A five-kilometer trip needs reliable suits and life support for all four; low gravity, rough terrain, dust, radiation, and temperature extremes add risk. The distant shelter’s condition and supplies are unknown. Evacuate only if the station can no longer sustain the crew and the route and destination have been checked.

Interlude / The Moon in music

Two songs, two lunar moods.

“Fly Me to the Moon” — Frank Sinatra

A polished, swinging performance that treats the Moon as part of a grand romantic journey. The mood is elegant and expansive, and the imagined trip suggests excitement and escape.

“Moondance” — Van Morrison

A relaxed, jazz-influenced song that uses moonlight as the setting for romance and dancing. Its mood feels warm and intimate rather than like a voyage through space.

Compare and contrast: Both songs connect the Moon with romance and use jazz-influenced sounds. “Fly Me to the Moon” emphasizes movement and a sweeping declaration; “Moondance” emphasizes atmosphere, rhythm, and a shared evening. One makes the Moon a destination; the other makes it a mood.
05 / Commander’s final transmission

Hour 71 · Mission log

Mission Control, this is Lunar Outpost One. At 03:17 on the first day, a micrometeoroid strike destroyed our oxygen-production system and damaged the solar array. We isolated the affected section, confirmed cabin pressure, and switched to stored oxygen. My hardest decision was refusing the proposed five-kilometer trek to the backup shelter. Our route and suits were not verified for that journey, while this habitat still had working pressure control and carbon-dioxide removal. We stayed together and moved to the central compartment when the radiation detector warned of rising levels, placing water containers and supply packs around us.

The Moon has almost no atmosphere to breathe and no global magnetic field to shield its surface from space radiation. Temperatures vary sharply between sunlight and shadow, so we kept thermal control running and avoided an unnecessary spacewalk. We shut down laboratory equipment, tracked our water and power, and sent repeated emergency messages on the backup radio. The crew is tired and anxious, but alert and supporting one another. Our oxygen and energy margins are narrow. I believe all four of us can survive if resupply arrives and we keep life support our first priority.

Commander’s final question

Why is the Moon harder to survive on than Earth’s harshest places?

Even Earth’s harshest environments still have a pressurized atmosphere and some protection from radiation; they also exist on a planet where help and supplies can eventually reach you. On the Moon, a small leak can become a life-threatening pressure loss, there is no air to breathe, and sunlight and shadow bring extreme temperature differences. Radiation and micrometeoroids reach the surface without Earth’s natural shielding, while sharp dust can damage equipment and threaten crew health. Distance makes rescue slower. Survival depends on a sealed habitat and several systems working together, not just human toughness. The crew must monitor resources, prepare for failures, and protect life support before anything else.
